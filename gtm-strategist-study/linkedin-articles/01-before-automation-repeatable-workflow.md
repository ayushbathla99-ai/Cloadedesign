# Before There's Automation, There's a Repeatable Workflow

*The Repeatability Ladder: a 5-rung test I run before any team builds a single AI agent*

---

Founder: "We want AI agents running our marketing by next quarter."

Me: "Love it. What's the one thing your best marketer does every single week, the same way?"

Founder: "Well… it depends on the week."

And there it is.

The sentence that quietly kills more AI marketing projects than bad tools, bad prompts, or bad budgets combined.

You can't automate *"it depends."*

---

In the last [N months], I've helped [N] teams install AI into their go-to-market: SaaS, agencies, and B2B service firms. The ones that got real leverage weren't the most technical or the best funded.

They were the ones who had something **repeatable** to hand over.

The rest bought tools, wired up a few automations, got "mid" output, and concluded that *AI doesn't work for marketing.*

It works. It just works on workflows, not on wishes.

So I started running every team through the same check before we build anything. I call it **the Repeatability Ladder**.

**In this article, you'll learn:**

- **The 5 rungs** a workflow climbs before an agent should touch it
- **The mistake teams make on each rung** (I've made most of them)
- **A 15-minute audit** to find your first agent-ready workflow this week

Let's climb.

---

## The Repeatability Ladder in one picture

| Rung | Name | What's true | Signal you're here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Improvised** | Someone does it when they remember | "It depends on the week" |
| 1 | **Done Twice** | It's been done at least twice, the same way | You can describe the steps out loud |
| 2 | **Written Down** | Steps, inputs, and a "good output" example exist | A new hire could do a passable v1 |
| 3 | **Rhythmic** | It runs on a schedule with a clear owner | It happens even when you're on holiday |
| 4 | **Delegated** | An agent drafts, a human reviews | Review takes < 20% of the old time |
| 5 | **Chained** | Its output feeds the next agent automatically | You only touch the exceptions |

Most teams try to jump from **Rung 0 to Rung 5** in one sprint.

That's the whole problem.

---

## Rung 1: Done Twice. Prove it isn't a one-off.

The first time you do something, you're exploring. The second time, you find out which parts actually matter.

**The mistake:** automating the first attempt. You end up encoding your experiment, including all the bits that didn't matter.

**Do this:** before you open any AI tool, do the task manually twice. Time it both times. If the steps changed a lot between round 1 and round 2, you're not ready. Do it a third time.

---

## Rung 2: Written Down. If a human can't follow it, an agent won't either.

Here's a test I use with every client: hand your written process to a smart freelancer who's never seen your business. Can they produce something you'd send?

If not, your agent will fail in exactly the same places, just faster and with more confidence.

A Rung-2 doc has four things:

1. **Trigger**: what starts it (e.g., "a sales call transcript lands in the folder")
2. **Inputs**: what it needs (ICP doc, voice guide, proof library)
3. **Steps**: in order, each one a verb
4. **A gold-standard example**: one output you'd happily publish

**The mistake:** writing *instructions* without an *example*. Agents learn far more from one great example than from ten paragraphs of rules.

---

## Rung 3: Rhythmic. Give it a heartbeat and an owner.

A workflow that runs "when we have time" is not a workflow. It's a hobby.

**Do this:** give it a cadence (daily, weekly, per event) and a single named owner. Not "marketing." A person.

This rung is boring. It is also where most of the value hides. Even *before* AI, a written, rhythmic workflow usually beats an improvised one by a mile.

---

## Rung 4: Delegated. The agent drafts, a human decides.

Now, and only now, hand it to an agent.

The agent gets your Rung-2 doc as its context: trigger, inputs, steps, gold example. The human's job shifts from *doing* to *reviewing*.

**The metric that matters:** review time. If reviewing the agent's output takes more than ~20% of the time the task used to take, something upstream is broken: usually the inputs (Rung 2) or the example.

**The mistake:** judging the agent on its first output. Judge it on its fifth, after you've fed your edits back into the doc.

---

## Rung 5: Chained. Outputs become inputs.

This is where the compounding starts.

A call-summary agent feeds a pain-point extractor. That feeds a content-angle agent, which feeds a drafting agent, which feeds a distribution scheduler. One customer conversation becomes a week of on-message content without anyone starting from a blank page.

In my own system, this is how [13] agents behave like one team rather than 13 chatbots. (Full breakdown in my [AI-Led Growth Playbook](link).)

**The mistake:** chaining workflows that are still on Rung 2. An error in step one doesn't stay in step one. It cascades.

## Copy this: the 15-minute ladder audit

| Marketing task | Times done the same way | Written down? (Y/N) | Cadence + owner? | Current rung | Next action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| e.g. Weekly customer story | 8 | Y | Fri · Priya | 3 | Delegate drafting |
| e.g. Competitor monitoring | 1 | N | — | 0 | Do it manually twice |
| … | | | | | |

**Rule:** your first agent goes to the **highest-rung, highest-frequency** task. Not the most exciting one.

---

**Where I stand:** AI doesn't create marketing systems. It amplifies the ones you already have, and that includes the chaos. Teams that spend two weeks climbing the ladder before building anything get to real leverage months faster than the ones who start with tools.

If AI disappeared tomorrow, would your marketing still run on a rhythm?

If the answer is no, that's your first project. Not the agent.
